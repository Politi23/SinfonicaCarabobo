Interview: The First Violinist on Preparing for Mahler's 5th

An intimate conversation about the emotional weight and technical demands of leading the string section through one of the most complex symphonies ever written.

Sarah Jenkins

October 24, 2023 • 5 min read

There is a moment of absolute silence before the baton drops. It is heavier than any note we play. For Elias Thorne, the First Violinist of our Symphony Orchestra, that silence is the culmination of months of preparation. "Mahler doesn't just ask you to play," Thorne explains, sitting in the quiet of the green room, his violin case resting shut on the table beside him. "He asks you to live through the music. Every bar is a confession."

We are discussing Gustav Mahler’s Symphony No. 5, a piece notorious for its emotional range—from the funeral march of the opening trumpet solo to the ecstatic chaos of the finale. For a concertmaster, the role is dual: he must be a soloist in his own right, yet a unifying force for the fifty string players behind him.

The Architecture of Sound

"The Fifth is architectural," Thorne says, gesturing with his hands as if shaping clay. "You have to understand the structure before you can worry about the emotion. If you get lost in the feeling too early, the building collapses." He describes his mornings: four hours of scales and études before he even opens the score. It is a regimen that would exhaust an athlete.

"You have to understand the structure before you can worry about the emotion. If you get lost in the feeling too early, the building collapses."

The famous Adagietto, often played as a standalone piece of mourning, is for Thorne a love letter. "It was written for Alma Mahler," he reminds me. "It shouldn't be a dirge. It should be intimate, like a whisper. Getting fifty people to whisper together? That is the challenge."

Leading from the Front

The relationship between the conductor and the first violinist is akin to a pilot and co-pilot. While the conductor sets the destination, the first violinist steers the plane. Thorne laughs when I suggest this. "Maybe," he says. "Or maybe the conductor is the architect, and I am the foreman. I have to make sure the bricks are laid straight."

As the interview winds down, Thorne picks up his instrument. The wood glows under the warm lights of the dressing room. He doesn't play Mahler. Instead, he plays a simple open string, tuning it with obsessive precision. It is a reminder that before the symphony can soar, the foundation must be perfect.
